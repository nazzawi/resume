Ask me an interview question

The behavioral questions TPM interviews ask. Pick one and you'll get the short answer I'd give in the room, and a link to the full story behind it.

"Walk me through your background."

I was hired at Snap in 2017 as an Arabic content analyst, reviewing content from one of our biggest markets, the Arab world. I noticed the design team building AR Lenses had no real sense of what to make for that market. I did, so I started working with them on a hijab Lens. It wasn't my job. It became a big part of my job, and it's how I moved full time into AR.

From there I spent six years as a TPM in AR: running moderation and content understanding for Lenses, partnering on 50,000+ Lens launches and 400+ Lens Studio and new AR tech launches, managing advertiser campaigns, and getting new products like GenAI Lenses and Easy Lens ready for moderation before they shipped. After Snap I spent four months at EA on product design, and I'm finishing my PMP now.

Read the origin story →

"Tell me about a time you disagreed with your team."

For Eid Al-Fitr 2022, the team planned to launch our Eid Lenses only in the Arab world. They saw Eid as a regional holiday. It isn't. Muslims celebrate it everywhere, and most Muslims don't live in the Arab world.

I explained it over and over. What finally worked was data from our previous launches, plus current figures on where the world's Muslim population lives, coming from someone they'd already shipped a lot of launches with. We launched globally, and it became Snap AR's biggest single event at 2.9B impressions.

Read the full story →

"Tell me about a trade-off between growth and safety."

Easy Lens allows creators to quickly edit and submit Lenses from templates. Working with Policy and Legal, we limited what the templates could change, so none of them could be used to mimic harmful stereotypes. The designers pushed back a bit, since it meant less creative freedom.

But fewer ways to change a Lens meant fewer ways to abuse it, which meant we could approve more Lenses, faster. Less freedom in each Lens, more Lenses actually getting through.

Read the full story →

"Tell me about a failure."

Our whole review workflow depended on one tool our vendor team overseas used to open and review Lenses. That tool was fragile. It broke now and then, and the vendor team reported bugs to us all the time. We had a new tool built and tested, but we were taking our time moving over.

Then one weekend the old tool stopped working completely. We tried everything and nothing worked, so we force-launched the new tool in the middle of the outage. Against a one-hour review goal, we were down for a couple of days. The failure wasn't the bug. It was knowing the old tool was fragile and not moving off it on our own schedule.

Read the full story →

"Walk me through a system you owned."

I owned Snap's content understanding for Lenses, end to end. For Creator Lenses, our vendor team tagged what was in each Lens. A Batman Lens gets tagged superhero, cartoon and movie, so people looking for superhero Lenses can actually find it in Search.

For Advertiser Lenses, the same team broke down each Lens's technical features and creative elements. That fed dashboards that Brand Insights and Sales used to tell brands like Nike and L'Oréal what makes a Lens work.

"How do you plan for risk?"

I assume everything will go wrong and have a mitigation for all of it. I learned that the hard way during a review-tool outage.

For Easy Lens, volumes were projected to grow by 10x, maybe 100x. Our usual goal was reviewing 90% of Lenses within an hour. Instead of promising that and breaking, we planned for the catastrophe ahead of time: a 24–48 hour target for Easy Lens, limits on what templates could change, and LLM-assisted review to absorb the projected volume.

"Tell me about working with senior leadership."

Ahead of Easy Lens, I worked with Snap's CTO on an ongoing basis so he understood what our moderation could actually handle. The goal was SLAs that were realistic without compromising our business metrics. That's how we landed on 24–48 hours for Easy Lens instead of our usual one hour.

I also reported our OKRs for review quality, turnaround time, handling time and capacity in weekly and quarterly business reviews.

"What does ownership mean to you?"

On the content understanding platforms, I owned everything: the taxonomy, quality, OKRs, tooling, vendor access to every Lens, and the engineering builds for policy and taxonomy changes.

Because I owned all of it, it was my responsibility to fix it. That's what ownership means to me.

"Tell me about a project you didn't finish."

Easy Lens was the last product I worked on at Snap. We'd planned the moderation for it: limited templates, a separate SLA, and LLM-assisted review to handle the volume. I left before the LLM review work was finished, so I didn't get to see whether it did what we designed it to do. I'd love to know.

"What would you do differently?"

Twice, on the Hijab Lens and Eid Al-Fitr, I was right about something the team doubted, and I didn't get credit when it worked. If I did it again, I'd make sure my part in the process was more visible.

Being right isn't enough. You have to make your role visible while the work is happening, not after. I'd also have built the skills to move into a TPM role sooner.

Getting moderation ready for Easy LensSnap · 2025 · Launch readiness for a GenAI product Launch readinessRisk managementExec alignment
My role Managed moderation readiness: scoped the backend work with engineers, wrote and cut the Jira tickets, and worked with the CTO on capacity and SLAs.

The problem

Easy Lens was a standalone app that let creators quickly edit and submit AR Lenses from templates, without building them from scratch. Making Lenses easier to make means more Lenses to review. Volume was projected to grow 10x, maybe 100x, and we were worried it would overwhelm our moderation systems.

Decisions

  1. Use LLMs to help with review. Instead of only adding reviewers, we started building an LLM-assisted review to absorb the volume.
  2. Limit what templates could change. With Policy and Legal, we limited template edits so no Lens could be used to mimic harmful stereotypes. The app and template designers pushed back a bit. But fewer ways to change a Lens meant fewer ways to abuse it, so we could approve more Lenses in a shorter window.
  3. A different SLA for a different product. Our normal goal was a one-hour p90: 90% of Lenses reviewed within an hour. For Easy Lens we planned for the catastrophe ahead of time and agreed on 24–48 hours instead, so our systems could keep up.

Working with the CTO

We worked with him on an ongoing basis so he understood what our capabilities actually were, and so we landed on SLAs that were realistic without compromising our business metrics.

The honest part Easy Lens was the last product I worked on before I left Snap. I didn't see it all the way through, including the LLM review work.
Eid Al-Fitr 2022: launching globallySnap AR · 2022 · Convincing a doubtful team Stakeholder influenceGlobal launch2.9B impressions
My role Owned timing, markets, review and localization, and worked with the design team on the Lenses.

The problem

The team planned to launch our Eid Lenses only in the Arab world. They saw Eid as a regional holiday. It isn't. Muslims celebrate it everywhere, and most Muslims don't live in the Arab world.

What made it hard

The team didn't have that context, and they didn't have much confidence that I was right. I explained it over and over.

What changed their minds

Two things. The data from our previous launches, plus current figures on where the world's Muslim population actually lives. And me: I'd worked on so many launches with this team that once the facts were in front of them, they trusted it.

Decision: a launch window, not a launch date

Eid starts with the moon sighting, so there's no fixed date to launch on. We ran the Lenses from two days before the expected sighting to three days after, so every market had them live whenever Eid started there.

Outcome

We launched globally. Eid Al-Fitr 2022 reached 2.9B impressions, the biggest single-event result Snap AR had ever had.

The honest part I was right, and I didn't get the credit. That was the second time, and it's when I learned that being right isn't enough. You have to make your role visible while the work is happening, not after.

When the whole review workflow stoppedSnap AR · Incident · A failure I own Incident responseRiskFailure

The problem

Our vendor moderators overseas suddenly couldn't open Lenses in the review tool. That was the entire workflow I owned. If they couldn't open a Lens, nothing got reviewed. The risk to the business was catastrophic. It would've compromised our entire moderation system. Our goal was a one-hour p90. It broke while I was out sick, and it stayed down for a couple of days through the weekend.

The warning signs were always there

The workflow was very hardware dependent, and the team was on another continent. Volume, internet connection, device updates, the weather, the Lens itself, bugs in the tool: it could go wrong in so many places. The tool had issues now and then, and the vendor team reported bugs to us regularly.

We had a new version of the tool, and we'd tested it, but we hadn't fully moved over. We were taking our time.

What we tried

Everything. We troubleshot every part of it with engineering. Everything failed.

Decision: force-launch the new tool

With nothing else working, we force-launched the new tool early as the workaround. The root cause turned out to be the old tool itself: it couldn't keep up with our volume anymore. It was fixed on Monday.

The honest part We knew the old tool was fragile and were taking our time moving off it. In the end we did the move anyway, just in the middle of an outage instead of on our own schedule. Now I plan for everything to go wrong and have a mitigation for all of it. Easy Lens is where I put that into practice.

How I work

Four things I learned the hard way, each from a story above.

Bring the context the room is missing

The Hijab Lens and Eid Al-Fitr both happened because I knew something the team didn't, and said it out loud.

Plan for everything to go wrong

Have a mitigation for all of it, before you need it. The review-tool outage taught me that.

If I own it, anything off is mine

Taxonomy, tooling, vendors, OKRs. No handing problems back over the wall.

Make the work visible as it happens

Being right isn't enough. I learned that from not getting credit, twice.
